Pricing a pre-purchase inspection

There are two prices on your books and they disagree by half. The site sells a comprehensive pre-purchase inspection at 200,000. The plan written on 15 September still anchors the market at 400,000. Below is the structure the business spec actually sets out, and the three levers that decide which number you can hold.

The price ladder, as the spec sets it

Five services, launched low to buy volume and then raised once there is a track record. Each price is fixed and disclosed before you start, and the customer pays after reading the report.

Four add-on products, priced as proposals to test

Mobile call-out adds 40,000 to 70,000 because travel is a real cost, not a rounding error. Post-quote verification, checking one garage's claim, is 50,000 to 80,000. Post-repair verification, did the fix actually work, is 60,000. Complex electrical work needing the Stage 2 kit is 250,000 to 500,000, and only once that kit exists.

The one launch offer, and it closes

A single time-boxed cohort at 50,000 for a full diagnostic inspection, for the first ten jobs only. Branded as a launch cohort, closed permanently afterwards. That is customer acquisition spend, not your price.

What one comprehensive job actually keeps

On a 200,000 inspection: fuel 20,000, admin and airtime 10,000, partner mechanic 30,000, equipment and consumables 10,000. That leaves 130,000 contribution. That is the number you defend, not the headline price. The partner mechanic is per job, never a second salary.

Break-even is low, which is the honest case for starting

Add up your fixed month: data and airtime, transport, mobile money charges, printing, insurance. At 600,000 a month of fixed cost, break-even is about five comprehensive inspections. Capacity caps you at two or three a day, so the constraint on income is jobs per month, and the plan treats that as a marketing problem.

What the machine has to repay

At 130,000 contribution per job: about 37 jobs if your 4.83 million order is genuine, about 85 jobs at the official retail price of roughly 11 million. The gap between those two is the real cost of getting procurement wrong.

The three levers that decide which price holds

Validate, do not assume

The spec itself says treat these figures as a structure to validate against your first paid jobs and against what local operators charge, then lock them. The 400,000 anchor is one reading of the market from 13 September; the 200,000 is what you published. Pick one and write down why.

Segment the price

A private buyer is price-sensitive and is your volume. A fleet, a financier or a diaspora buyer is buying trust and pays more. One flat price across all five client types leaves money on the table at the top end and loses volume at the bottom.

Protect the contribution

The plan's own instruction is optimise contribution per job, not headline revenue. A 400,000 price that drags in a partner mechanic, a long drive and a re-inspection can keep less than a clean 200,000 job does.

Which pricing line do you want to run?

My recommendation: hold the comprehensive inspection at 200,000 for the launch cohort, raise it to 300,000 once you have ten written reports to show, and price fleets and financiers separately from the start. Which way do you want me to take it?
